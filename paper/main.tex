\begin{abstract}
Token-level adaptive compute promises to make the transformer forward pass
resource-aware, but most mechanisms rely on \emph{endogenous} signals---attention
entropy, router scores, confidence estimates---whose correlation with problem
difficulty is assumed rather than tested. We test the assumption on arithmetic
with graded difficulty, a clean testbed: difficulty grades deterministically
from 1-digit addition to $4{\times}3$-digit multiplication, ground-truth labels
exist for every token, and there are no confounds from pretraining knowledge.
The endogenous signals fail in three distinct ways: an entropy-driven gate
\emph{collapses} to a constant
(per-token entropy--difficulty correlation $r \approx -0.06$); a tag-initialized
gate \emph{inverts}, allocating more compute to easy problems than hard ones
($r = \CorrIterII$) because the cross-entropy gradient is largest on
memorizable tokens; and a learned difficulty head trained without supervision is
\emph{vacuous}, with unstable allocation across seeds. Explicit per-token
difficulty supervision with a budget constraint fixes all three: gates order
correctly (easy $<$ medium $<$ hard) in every seed with per-problem
gate--difficulty $r = \CorrIterIV$, accuracy matches the full-compute
baseline while cutting FLOPs per token by \OursTSave\%, and it beats a
static schedule at the same compute budget. When difficulty labels or
proxies exist, supervised allocation is simple, reliable, and interpretable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Resource-aware agentic AI is mostly practiced \emph{outside} the model: cascade
queries across models of different sizes \citep{chen2023frugalgpt}, truncate
chains of thought, cap tool calls, budget retries. The forward pass itself
remains resource-blind: a transformer \citep{vaswani2017attention} spends
identical FLOPs on a trivial token and on the token that determines whether the
answer is right. Yet difficulty is unevenly distributed across tokens---carrying
a digit in multi-digit multiplication is hard; copying a ``$+$'' sign is not. An
agent that must meter its compute should be able to allocate it per token.

Adaptive-compute methods show that large savings are available: early exiting
\citep{teerapittayanon2016branchynet,xin2020deebert,schuster2022calm}, adaptive
computation time \citep{graves2016act}, dynamic layer skipping
\citep{wang2018skipnet,elbayad2020depth}, slimmable networks
\citep{yu2019universally}, and token-level Mixture-of-Depths (MoD) routing
\citep{raposo2024mixture} all reduce average compute per token. Nearly all of
them share one assumption: that a signal the network already produces
internally---attention entropy, router scores, exit confidence---correlates with
how difficult a token is, so that end-to-end training on the task loss will shape
that signal into a sensible allocation. This assumption is rarely tested
directly. We test it, and it fails.

This paper is an empirical account of a four-iteration journey toward token-level
adaptive compute on arithmetic reasoning with graded difficulty
(Table~\ref{tab:journey}). Our testbed is a per-token continuous gate
$g \in [0,1]$ that scales each token's feed-forward (FFN) output at every layer,
with FLOPs charged proportionally to the gate: a token gated at $0.2$ costs a
fifth of the FFN compute of a token gated at $1.0$. The question is what should
drive the gate. Iteration~1 drives it with attention entropy, a label-free
endogenous difficulty proxy: the gate collapses to a constant and entropy turns
out not to encode task difficulty ($r \approx -0.06$ per token). Iteration~2
initializes the gating state from explicit difficulty tags: the gate now varies,
but \emph{inverts}---easy tokens receive more compute than hard ones
($r = \CorrIterII$ per problem)---because the cross-entropy gradient is
largest on easy, memorizable tokens and end-to-end training follows the gradient,
not the semantics. Iteration~3 replaces the entropy signal with a learned
difficulty head but still trains it only on the task loss: the gate is nearly
flat and its correlation with difficulty is unstable across seeds. Iteration~4
supervises the head explicitly with per-token difficulty targets plus a budget
constraint, and it works.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A systematic diagnosis of endogenous difficulty signals.}
  Across three distinct instantiations (entropy-driven state, tag-initialized
  state, learned unsupervised head), endogenous training signals fail in three
  different ways---\emph{collapse}, \emph{inversion}, and \emph{vacuity}
  (\S\ref{sec:failures}). A standard MoD top-$k$ router exhibits the same
  inversion, suggesting the pathology is a general property of endogenous
  training dynamics, not of our architecture.
\item \textbf{A simple fix: explicit difficulty supervision with a budget
  constraint} (\S\ref{sec:method}). A small per-layer difficulty head is trained
  with an MSE loss toward tag-derived per-token targets while a budget term pins
  the mean gate; a two-stage protocol with a nearly frozen body preserves task
  accuracy.
\item \textbf{A controlled study} on tagged arithmetic (\S\ref{sec:tagged}):
  gates order correctly (easy $<$ medium $<$ hard) in all 3 seeds with
  per-problem gate--difficulty $r = \CorrIterIV$; accuracy matches the
  full-compute baseline at \OursTSave\% fewer FLOPs per token, and
  exceeds a static gate schedule at the same compute budget.
\item \textbf{An analysis of generalization} (\S\ref{sec:mixed},
  \S\ref{sec:ablations}): on untagged mixed-difficulty inputs a coarse
  digit-count proxy partially transfers (correct ordering, $r = 0.70$, small
  spread), and we identify when a static schedule suffices.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Adaptive compute.} Adaptive Computation Time
\citep{graves2016act} lets recurrent networks ponder per position, with a
halting unit trained end-to-end. Early-exit methods attach classifiers to
intermediate layers and stop when confident
\citep{teerapittayanon2016branchynet,xin2020deebert,elbayad2020depth,schuster2022calm}.
SkipNet \citep{wang2018skipnet} learns per-example layer skipping, and
universally slimmable networks
\citep{yu2019universally} run a single network at several widths.
Mixture-of-Depths \citep{raposo2024mixture} routes tokens through or around
transformer blocks with a learned top-$k$ router, allocating a fixed compute
budget per sequence. All of these decide allocation from endogenous
quantities---halting scores, exit confidence, router logits---trained on the
task loss (sometimes with auxiliary balancing objectives). Our work is
complementary: rather than proposing a new routing mechanism, we ask whether
such endogenous signals actually track problem difficulty, and show that in our
setting they do not unless supervised. MoD's own router turns out to exhibit
the inversion we diagnose (\S\ref{sec:ablations}).

\paragraph{Conditional computation and mixture-of-experts.}
Sparsely-gated MoE \citep{shazeer2017outrageously} routes tokens to expert FFNs
via a learned gating network, primarily to scale capacity at constant compute
rather than to allocate by difficulty; load-balancing losses are needed to
prevent collapse to a few experts---an early sign that end-to-end router
dynamics do not self-organize into the intended allocation. Our DifficultyHead
is easy to mistake for this kind of routing, so it is worth separating the two:
MoE dispatches each token to a subset of expert parameters, changing
\emph{which} capacity processes it, whereas our head changes \emph{how much}
compute the token receives and leaves the parameters in place. Attaching a
difficulty predictor to expert selection is a natural combination; we leave it
to future work.

\paragraph{Entropy and confidence as uncertainty proxies.}
Attention entropy varies systematically across tokens and heads
\citep{clark2019bert,vig2019analyzing}, and predictive confidence is widely
used as a difficulty or calibration proxy \citep{guo2017calibration}, e.g.\ for
early exits. We test attention entropy directly as a \emph{task-difficulty}
signal and find it nearly uncorrelated with ground-truth difficulty
($r \approx -0.06$ per token; \S\ref{sec:failures}).

\paragraph{Resource-aware agents.}
Orchestration-level resource awareness---model cascades and routing
\citep{chen2023frugalgpt}, token and tool budgets---treats each model call as
fixed-cost. Token-level gating makes the call itself cost-sensitive. Our results
bear on when such metering is feasible: when difficulty labels or proxies exist
(curriculum tags, verifier scores, metadata), supervised allocation is cheap and
reliable; when only endogenous signals are available, allocation may be absent,
inverted, or unstable.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Architecture and per-token gating}
\label{sec:arch}

Our backbone is an 11M-parameter decoder-only transformer: $d_{\mathrm{model}} =
384$, 6 layers, 6 attention heads, $d_{\mathrm{ff}} = 1536$, vocabulary 22,
sequence length 256. Each layer computes
\begin{equation}
x_2 = \LN\!\bigl(x_1 + \attn(x_1)\bigr), \qquad
x_3 = \LN\!\bigl(x_2 + g_t \cdot \FFN(x_2)\bigr),
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
where $g_t \in [0,1]$ is a per-token, per-layer gate. Compute is charged
proportionally: a position gated at $g$ is billed $g$ times the per-token FFN
cost, while attention cost is always paid in full. A gate below $1$ thus saves
FLOPs proportionally (about \OursTSave\% of total per-token FLOPs at
mean gate $\approx 0.5$, since the FFN dominates the layer budget).

In our final method, the gate is produced by a small per-layer
\emph{DifficultyHead}: a two-layer MLP
($\mathrm{Linear}(384{\to}96)$, ReLU, $\mathrm{Linear}(96{\to}1)$) applied to the
post-attention hidden state $x_2$, whose scalar output $s_t$ is squashed by a
sigmoid, $g_t = \sigma(s_t)$. Earlier iterations used other drivers for the gate
(attention entropy propagated through a low-dimensional recurrent ``chemical
state'', or a tag-initialized state); \S\ref{sec:failures} describes them and
their failure modes. All variants share Eq.~\eqref{eq:gate} and the FLOPs
accounting, so comparisons isolate the gating signal.

\subsection{Why endogenous signals fail: three diagnostics}
\label{sec:methodfail}

End-to-end training offers the gate only one channel of feedback: the task loss.
We identify three ways this goes wrong, developed empirically in
\S\ref{sec:failures}.
\emph{Collapse}: if the driving signal carries no information about difficulty,
the cheapest way to satisfy a sparsity penalty is a uniform gate---and attention
entropy is such a signal ($r \approx -0.06$ per token).
\emph{Inversion}: a ground-truth initialization makes the gate vary, but easy
problems are memorizable, so their cross-entropy gradient is largest and
training allocates them \emph{more} compute.
\emph{Vacuity}: a head trained only through the task loss receives no signal
about what difficulty is, so the gate stays flat and unstable.
\S\ref{sec:supervised} supplies both the missing signal and the incentive.

\subsection{Explicit difficulty supervision with a budget constraint}
\label{sec:supervised}

Our method trains the DifficultyHeads with explicit per-token targets derived
from difficulty metadata. Training is two-stage.

\textbf{Stage 1 (task training).} The transformer is trained conventionally for
5000 steps with the gate fixed at $1$ everywhere, and checkpointed. This
decouples representation learning from allocation learning.

\textbf{Stage 2 (allocation training).} Starting from the checkpoint, we attach
the per-layer DifficultyHeads and train for 3000 steps with
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} \;=\; \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}
\;+\; \lambda_{\mathrm{mse}}\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t}\bigl(\sigma(s_t) - \tau_t\bigr)^2
\;+\; \lambda_{\mathrm{budget}}\,\bigl(\bar{g} - 0.5\bigr)^2,
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_t$ is a per-position target gate and $\bar{g}$ the mean gate over the
batch. On tagged data, targets come from the difficulty tag: easy $\to 0.2$,
medium $\to 0.5$, hard $\to 0.8$, and non-problem positions $\to 0.5$. We use
$\lambda_{\mathrm{mse}} = 5.0$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{budget}} = 0.5$; the budget
term pins the mean gate near $0.5$ (hence the total compute bill) while leaving
the head free to redistribute compute \emph{across} tokens. There is no sparsity
penalty: the goal is allocation, not maximal savings. Stage 2 uses a two-group
AdamW schedule: the body trains at a near-frozen learning rate $3{\times}10^{-6}$
and the DifficultyHeads at $3{\times}10^{-4}$; gradient clipping is $1.0$
throughout. The low body learning rate preserves Stage-1 accuracy while the
heads learn allocation; the cost is that the body can no longer adapt much in
Stage 2, a confound we return to in \S\ref{sec:mixed} and \S\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Tasks.} We study arithmetic with graded difficulty in two regimes. In
the \emph{tagged} regime each problem is prefixed with a difficulty tag:
\texttt{[E]} 1-digit addition/subtraction (${\sim}162$ unique problems,
memorizable), \texttt{[M]} 3-digit addition/subtraction (${\sim}1.6$M unique),
and \texttt{[H]} 4-digit ${\times}$ 3-digit multiplication (${\sim}9$M unique).
In the \emph{mixed} regime there are no tags: operands have 1--4 digits and
operators are $\{+, -, \times\}$; the difficulty bin is the digit count of the
larger operand (1--4). Sequences pack multiple problems up to 256 tokens.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We use a frozen held-out set of 4000 problems per
difficulty bin (seed 12345, identical across methods and seeds), report accuracy
on answer tokens only, and run 3 seeds for every method. We report raw and
balanced (macro over bins) accuracy, MFLOPs per token under the accounting of
\S\ref{sec:arch}, the mean gate per difficulty bin, and the gate spread
(hardest minus easiest bin).

\textbf{Baselines.} All baselines train end-to-end for 6000 steps:
\emph{Baseline} (standard transformer), \emph{Chemical-off} (gating pathway
frozen, gate $=1$; an architecture-matched full-compute control),
\emph{Fixed-schedule} (gate fixed at $0.5$ for every token; a static schedule at
the same budget as ours), \emph{MoD} (top-$k$ token routing
\citep{raposo2024mixture} at matched capacity), \emph{Chemical-entropy}
(iteration~1: entropy-driven chemical-state gate with sparsity penalty),
\emph{Random-gate} (entropy replaced by uniform noise in the same range), and
\emph{Tag-only} (gate driven only by the difficulty-tag identity---a static
oracle heuristic). Our method (\emph{Difficulty-supervised}) uses the two-stage
protocol of \S\ref{sec:supervised} (5000$+$3000 steps on tagged). All models
share the 11M backbone, batch size 32, AdamW, and gradient clipping $1.0$.

\paragraph{Calibration.} Baselines train 6000 steps end-to-end while ours trains
5000$+$3000 with a nearly frozen body in Stage 2; comparisons below are ``same
task, similar total budget'', not a controlled iso-FLOPs training study.

\subsection{Diagnosing the failure modes}
\label{sec:failures}

Table~\ref{tab:journey} summarizes the journey; Figure~\ref{fig:correlation}
quantifies the allocation quality per iteration.

\textbf{Iteration 1: attention entropy collapses.} A per-token low-dimensional
chemical state, updated from position-normalized attention entropy and mapped to
the gate, trained end-to-end with cross-entropy plus a sparsity penalty,
converges to a constant gate: mean gates for E/M/H are $0.232/0.233/0.233$
(spread $\approx 0.001$). Accuracy is fine---$71.5{\pm}1.3$\% at $11.5$
MFLOPs/token---so the model saves compute, but it does not allocate
differentially. The reason is the signal, not the mechanism: the per-token
Pearson correlation between attention entropy and difficulty is $\approx -0.06$
(seed range $+0.05$ to $-0.12$). Attention entropy does not encode task
difficulty in this setting.

\textbf{Iteration 2: tag initialization inverts.} Initializing the chemical
state from the difficulty-tag embedding gives the pathway ground-truth
information at the start of training. The gate does go live---but inverted:
E/M/H gates are $0.266/0.265/0.213$, and the per-problem correlation between
mean gate and difficulty bin is $\CorrIterII$, negative in all 3 seeds.
The cause is the training dynamics, not the initialization: the cross-entropy
gradient is largest on easy (memorizable) tokens, so end-to-end training pushes
compute where the loss is most reducible, not where the problem is hardest.

\textbf{Iteration 3: a learned head without supervision is vacuous.} Replacing
the entropy pathway with the DifficultyHead of \S\ref{sec:arch}---two-stage
protocol (Stage 1 full FFN for 3000 steps; Stage 2 head trained only by
cross-entropy)---yields a nearly flat gate: E/M/H $= 0.812/0.824/0.834$, spread
$0.02$. The per-problem gate--difficulty correlation is unstable across seeds
($r = 0.65/0.64/0.04$). Without an explicit signal, there is no reliable
allocation.

\textbf{Iteration 4: explicit supervision works.} With the loss of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}, gates order correctly in every seed; we develop this result
next. The contrast across iterations is the point: the failure is not in any one
mechanism but in the assumption that endogenous training will discover the
allocation semantics on its own.

\subsection{Mixed arithmetic: partial generalization without tags}
\label{sec:mixed}

In the mixed regime there are no difficulty tags, so we supervise the head with
a coarse proxy: digit count of the larger operand, mapping bins 1/2/3/4 to
targets $0.2/0.4/0.6/0.8$. (The mixed runs use a 3000-step Stage 1; only the
tagged headline runs use 5000 steps.) The result is a partial success
(Table~\ref{tab:mixed}, Appendix~\ref{app:mixed}). The allocation is correct in
direction: mean gates per bin are $0.45/0.50/0.52/0.53$, monotone increasing,
with per-problem $r = 0.70$---but the spread is small ($0.08$), and accuracy is
$58.5{\pm}3.4$\% ($69.9{\pm}2.7$ balanced) at \OursMFlops\ MFLOPs/token,
well below the 6000-step baselines ($85.0{\pm}1.7$ for the standard baseline).

Context matters for interpreting this gap. Our mixed Stage-1-only checkpoint
(3000 steps, full FFN, ungated) scores just $53.1$\% at $33.2$ MFLOPs/token: the
gated Stage-2 model \emph{beats its own ungated starting point} by $5.4$ points
while using \OursMSave\% fewer FLOPs. Because the body is nearly frozen
in Stage 2 (learning rate $3{\times}10^{-6}$), most of the gap to the baselines
is a training-budget artifact rather than a cost of gating. We therefore claim
generalization only in a limited sense: a coarse difficulty proxy suffices to
produce a correctly ordered, positively correlated allocation on untagged
inputs, but the allocation is shallow and the accuracy deficit is real.
\S\ref{sec:discussion} returns to what would be needed to close it.

\subsection{Comparisons and ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}

\textbf{Static schedules.} The Fixed-schedule baseline answers the question
``why not just shrink the FFN uniformly?'' At matched budget on tagged data it
loses $\sim$2 accuracy points to our method (Table~\ref{tab:tagged}); on mixed
data it is strong ($86.6{\pm}0.9$ @ $19.0$M), indicating that when per-token
difficulty information is weak or unavailable, a static schedule is a
surprisingly competitive default (see \S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\textbf{MoD inverts too.} The inversion pathology is not specific to our
architecture. A standard MoD top-$k$ router \citep{raposo2024mixture} allocates
a slightly \emph{larger} fraction of its compute budget to easy tokens than to
hard ones (fraction of tokens processed, E/M/H $= 0.135/0.109/0.093$), and its
accuracy suffers ($62.6{\pm}4.8$ tagged, $70.5{\pm}2.9$ mixed). Endogenous
training dynamics push compute toward reducible error regardless of the routing
mechanism. MoD is thus not a counterexample to our diagnosis but its most
prominent instance: the canonical token-level allocator rests on the same
untested assumption, and in our setting it fails the same way.

\textbf{Controls.} Random-gate ($70.7{\pm}4.4$) and Tag-only ($71.6{\pm}3.1$)
show that neither gate variance alone nor a static oracle tag heuristic explains
our result; Chemical-off ($74.4{\pm}0.7$) confirms the gating pathway itself
costs nothing when disabled.

\textbf{Stage-1 length.} Ablating Stage 1 from 5000 to 3000 steps (same
protocol otherwise) drops tagged accuracy to $66.9{\pm}2.1$ @ $19.6$
MFLOPs/token (Table~\ref{tab:tagged}, ``Ours (Stage-1 = 3K)''): the body needs
enough Stage-1 training before the allocation layer is added, consistent with
the mixed-regime analysis of \S\ref{sec:mixed}.

\textbf{Frontier.} Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} plots accuracy against MFLOPs per
token for all methods on both regimes. On tagged data our method sits on the
Pareto frontier at the high-accuracy end---the only adaptive method that matches
full-compute accuracy at substantially reduced compute.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Implications for resource-aware agents.} Our experiments use
arithmetic as a controlled testbed, but the core finding---endogenous
difficulty signals fail, and explicit supervision is required---should carry to
any agentic reasoning loop whose tokens differ in computational demand, from
tool-use trajectories to code generation. The practical lesson is
about where difficulty knowledge comes from. Agentic pipelines often possess
difficulty metadata that end-to-end training cannot discover on its own:
curriculum tags in training corpora, verifier or reward-model scores, problem
metadata, or cheap proxy features such as operand length. Our results show that
spending this metadata on explicit per-token supervision yields an allocation
that is reliable (correct ordering in every seed), interpretable (gates can be
read directly as a compute budget per token), and cheap (a two-layer head per
transformer layer). Conversely, relying on signals the network already
produces---entropy, router scores---risks allocation that is absent, inverted,
or unstable, and the failure can be silent: iteration~1 saved 65\% of FLOPs with
healthy accuracy while allocating nothing differentially. Inversion is the more
dangerous failure than collapse: it actively misallocates while appearing to
work.

\paragraph{When static schedules suffice.} On mixed data the fixed schedule at
the same budget beat every adaptive method (Table~\ref{tab:mixed}). Adaptive
allocation pays when (i) difficulty varies substantially across tokens,
(ii) a reliable difficulty signal or proxy exists, and (iii) the base model is
already competent. When any of these fails---weak proxy (mixed regime),
undertrained body (short Stage 1)---a uniform schedule is a strong baseline that
adaptive methods must beat, not merely undercut on FLOPs.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Our study is deliberately narrow: a single task family
(arithmetic), a single 11M-parameter model, and difficulty defined by operand
size; whether the same failure modes and fixes hold at scale and on natural
language is open. FLOPs are an accounting of skippable compute: under dense
execution the wallclock is unchanged, and realizing the savings requires sparse
or gated kernels. The mixed-regime gap is partly a training-budget artifact, and
our tagged comparisons are same-task, similar-budget rather than iso-FLOPs
controlled. Our targets are hand-set ($0.2/0.5/0.8$); learning them, or
deriving them from measured per-problem loss, is a natural next step.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We tested the assumption underlying most token-level adaptive-compute methods:
that endogenous signals correlate with problem difficulty. On arithmetic with
graded difficulty, they do not---failing by collapse, inversion, or vacuity
across three distinct mechanisms, with a standard MoD router inverting as well.
Explicit per-token difficulty supervision with a budget constraint fixes the
allocation: correct ordering in every seed, per-problem $r =
\CorrIterIV$, full-compute accuracy at \OursTSave\% fewer
FLOPs per token, and a win over the same-budget static schedule. For
resource-aware agents the message is conservative but useful: when difficulty
labels or proxies exist, supervised token-level allocation is a simple,
reliable, interpretable metering primitive; when they do not, endogenous
signals should not be trusted to allocate on their own.

\section{Experimental details}
\label{app:details}

\paragraph{Model and optimization.} All models share the 11M-parameter
decoder-only backbone of \S\ref{sec:arch} ($d_{\mathrm{model}}=384$, 6 layers, 6
heads, $d_{\mathrm{ff}}=1536$, vocab 22, sequence length 256). We use batch size
32, AdamW, and gradient clipping at $1.0$ throughout. Stage 2 uses two parameter
groups: body at $3{\times}10^{-6}$, DifficultyHeads at $3{\times}10^{-4}$. The
loss is Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} with $\lambda_{\mathrm{mse}}=5.0$,
$\lambda_{\mathrm{budget}}=0.5$, budget target $0.5$, and no sparsity penalty.
Tagged targets are E$\to 0.2$, M$\to 0.5$, H$\to 0.8$ with non-problem positions
at $0.5$; mixed targets map digit bins 1/2/3/4 to $0.2/0.4/0.6/0.8$.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.} The held-out set contains 4000 problems per
difficulty bin, generated once with seed 12345 and shared across all methods and
seeds. Accuracy is computed on answer tokens only; balanced accuracy is the
macro average over bins. Gate statistics are means over the layer-averaged gate
at problem positions. Reported correlations are per-problem Pearson $r$ between
the mean gate of a problem's positions and its difficulty bin, except where
noted as per-token (iteration~1's entropy analysis). All numbers are
mean$\pm$std over 3 seeds.

\paragraph{Per-difficulty details.} Per-bin accuracies for all methods are
visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:perdifficulty_acc}; per-bin gates in
Table~\ref{tab:journey} and Figure~\ref{fig:gate_ordering}. Gate spread is
defined as the hardest-bin mean gate minus the easiest-bin mean gate.
