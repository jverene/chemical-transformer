\begin{abstract}
Token-level adaptive computation is often motivated by local signals such as
entropy, confidence, or gate gradients. The key question is whether acting on
such a signal improves learning under an executable compute budget. In a
controlled arithmetic testbed, we find that endogenous gates collapse, invert,
or remain vacuous; difficulty supervision can order gates without improving
accuracy; and a first-order gate-gradient allocator underperforms random
allocation. These are failures of specific local procedures, not evidence of a
general inability to learn allocation.

We then test two alternatives using exact hard budgets and gather/scatter FFN
execution. A router optimized through realized AdamW updates reduces final
sparse-evaluation loss relative to random allocation by
$\MetaRandomDelta\pm\MetaRandomSE$ and relative to shuffled scores by
$\MetaShuffledDelta\pm\MetaShuffledSE$ on two confirmation models. A frozen
rule that executes tokens with the smallest pre-FFN maximum absolute activation
beats exact-budget random allocation in all eight model--budget regimes and at
all 32 evaluations. Averaging first within each model, it improves final loss
by $\ProgramLossDelta\pm\ProgramLossSE$ and answer accuracy by
$\ProgramAccuracyDelta\pm\ProgramAccuracySE$ percentage points. A 50-step
continuation pilot at 150M on two additional models and newly generated
held-out data also preserves the frozen rule's final sparse-loss advantage.

The benefits concern sparse execution: the online router is expensive, and its
gain does not translate into better dense-model quality. Neither method
establishes a replicated win over dense-fewer-steps at matched total cost. The
resulting study distinguishes which allocation objectives produce useful token
selections from the practical conditions needed to improve the
quality--compute frontier.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Transformers apply a feed-forward network (FFN) to every token in every layer.
Adaptive-computation methods instead try to spend that computation selectively.
The difficult question is not whether a score can be correlated with token
difficulty. It is whether acting on that score improves subsequent learning
under an executable compute budget.

Our starting point is a set of controlled failures of local allocation signals.
Entropy gates became nearly constant, tag-initialized gates inverted their
initial ordering, and unsupervised heads were unstable. Supervised gates could
recover a difficulty ordering, but shuffled targets produced nearly the same
task performance. An online gate-gradient allocator also performed worse than
random allocation. These failures do not imply that useful allocation is
unlearnable in general. A gate gradient measured with the model held fixed is
only a local linear estimate: it neither bounds the effect of a finite hard
mask nor captures how a model and allocator adapt together during training.

We therefore test two alternatives. First, we train a router through the actual
model update caused by its hard mask. Second, we ask whether a fixed activation
rule already contains reusable allocation structure. Both improve over random
allocation under sparse execution. To assess practical value, we compare them
with the strongest simple alternative: training a dense model for fewer steps.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item We provide a controlled failure analysis of endogenous and first-order
allocation. Shuffled-target controls distinguish recovering a difficulty
ordering from improving task outcomes, and we limit the conclusion to the
tested procedures.
\item We introduce an online objective based on realized body updates. A router
differentiated through functional AdamW updates beats random and shuffled
allocation at every reported checkpoint on two confirmation bodies under hard
50\% FFN execution.
\item We identify a cheap frozen alternative, \emph{low-max-abs}, that executes
the tokens with smallest pre-FFN maximum absolute activation. It improves over
random allocation across four bodies and two budgets without router learning,
lookahead batches, or a dense FFN planning pass. A short 150M pilot with two
additional bodies and new held-out data reproduces the final sparse-loss
benefit.
\item We separate allocation learnability from practical efficiency. Sparse and
dense evaluation are reported separately, routing and meta-learning work are
included in practical comparisons, and no method achieves a replicated win over
dense-fewer-steps at matched total cost.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Adaptive computation.}
Adaptive Computation Time and Universal Transformers learn input-dependent
halting or recurrence \citep{graves2016act,dehghani2019universal}. Early-exit
models and cascades make confidence-based depth decisions
\citep{teerapittayanon2016branchynet,xin2020deebert,elbayad2020depth,schuster2022calm}.
Mixture-of-Depths routes tokens through a fixed layer budget
\citep{raposo2024mixture}, while Mixture-of-Recursions combines token-dependent
recursive depth with parameter sharing and reports improved quality--compute
trade-offs from 135M to 1.7B parameters \citep{bae2025mixture}. These positive
results show that adaptive computation can succeed under appropriate designs.
Our question is narrower: which token-level FFN allocation signals and learning
objectives produce useful outcomes in a controlled testbed?

\citet{moosa2026dynamic} independently study complexity-controlled recurrent
Transformers. They find that complexity-aligned allocation can emerge without
explicit difficulty supervision, yet need not yield algorithmic
generalization. Our results concern a different action---executing an FFN row
inside a non-recurrent Transformer---but share their distinction between an
interpretable allocation pattern and useful model outcomes.

\paragraph{Learning through updates.}
Our online router uses hypergradients through an optimizer step, in the lineage
of gradient-based hyperparameter optimization and model-agnostic meta-learning
\citep{maclaurin2015gradient,finn2017maml}. The allocation decision is discrete
in the forward pass, so we use a straight-through gradient estimator
\citep{bengio2013estimating}. Unlike a gate-gradient saliency target, the
reward is the loss after a realized body update on subsequent ordinary training
batches. This directly tests persistent co-adaptation rather than a frozen-body
linearization.

\paragraph{Saliency, sparsity, and random controls.}
Gate gradients are related to Taylor saliency used in pruning
\citep{lecun1990obd,molchanov2019importance}, but they measure sensitivity at
the current body rather than the value of an allocation after retraining. Token
pruning and data selection make different decisions at inference or at the
dataset level \citep{rao2021dynamicvit,bolya2023tome,hou2022tokendropping,lin2024rho1}.
Our frozen program is instead an online FFN-execution rule. Random and
shuffled-mask controls are essential: random allocation matches the executed
budget, while shuffling preserves scores but destroys their association with
token states. The original dense-deployment dropout experiments belong to the
structured-dropout literature
\citep{srivastava2014dropout,huang2016stochastic,fan2019layerdrop}; they are not
evidence of adaptive inference efficiency and are reported separately in
Appendix~\ref{app:legacy}.

\section{Executable Allocation and Learning Objectives}
\label{sec:method}

\caption{Soft-gate diagnostics versus executable allocation. A fractional gate

\label{fig:execution}

\subsection{Continuous relaxations are not executed savings}
The original gate study used
\begin{equation}
x_3=\LN\!\left(x_2+g_{t,\ell}\FFN(x_2)\right),\qquad g_{t,\ell}\in[0,1].
\label{eq:softgate}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:softgate} is a useful differentiable relaxation, but a
nonzero fractional gate still computes the FFN. Billing it as $g_{t,\ell}$
times the FFN cost therefore does not describe realized operations
(Figure~\ref{fig:execution}). We use these gates only as allocation diagnostics.

\subsection{Exact hard execution}
All new allocation results use a binary mask. For valid, non-padding token
states $h_{t,\ell}$ before layer $\ell$'s FFN, a score $s_{t,\ell}$ determines
\begin{equation}
m_{t,\ell}=\mathbf{1}\!\left[s_{t,\ell}\in\TopB(s_{\cdot,\ell})\right],
\qquad
B_t=\min\!\left(n_t,\max\!\left(1,\operatorname{round}(r n_t)\right)\right),
\label{eq:hardmask}
\end{equation}
where $n_t$ is the number of valid positions in the sequence and $r$ is the
activity budget. Padding is excluded. The implementation gathers active rows,
computes the FFN only on those rows, and scatters the results back with zero
contribution elsewhere. Attention remains dense. Thus a 50\% mask saves actual
FFN matrix-multiplication work; it does not imply a 50\% reduction in total
training FLOPs.

\subsection{Shared continuation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
Unless stated otherwise, new experiments use the 11M tagged-arithmetic testbed:
six layers, $d_{\mathrm{model}}=384$, $d_{\mathrm{FFN}}=1536$, sequence length
256, and batch size 32. Difficulty tags distinguish easy and medium addition
from hard multiplication. We use \emph{body} to denote the model parameters at
a checkpoint; different body seeds provide distinct trained models. Every
comparison begins from a shared mid-training checkpoint after 3{,}000 steps and
continues for 200 AdamW updates with learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, weight
decay 0.01, dropout 0.1, and gradient clipping. Paired arms receive the same
pregenerated ordinary training stream. Sparse evaluation applies the same hard
FFN budget used during training; dense evaluation restores every FFN row.
Answer accuracy uses answer tokens only.

The learnability comparisons match the number of updates and the exact executed
FFN-row budget. Random allocation draws a new exact-budget mask. The shuffled
control permutes valid scores within each sequence and layer, preserving the
score multiset but destroying the association with token states. These
comparisons test allocation at fixed executed budget. They are not
cost-matched practical-efficiency comparisons.

\subsection{Online routing through realized AdamW updates}
\label{sec:meta-method}
A small router maps each current pre-FFN state, layer index, and position to a
score. Its forward decision is the hard mask in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hardmask}; a
sigmoid straight-through estimator supplies gradients. Every tenth step, we
construct a differentiable functional AdamW update
\begin{equation}
\theta_{t+1}(\phi)=U_{\mathrm{AdamW}}
\!\left(\theta_t,A_t,m_t(\phi)\right),
\qquad
R_t(\phi)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathcal L(A_{t+j};\theta_{t+1}(\phi)),
\label{eq:meta}
\end{equation}
and update router parameters $\phi$ by descending $\nabla_\phi R_t$. Here $M=4$.
The next four batches come from the ordinary training stream and are
subsequently used for body training, so they add no examples. Their forward
evaluations and all higher-order backward work are auxiliary computation.

The computed body parameters and optimizer moments are installed as the real
next state, and the higher-order graph is detached between steps. Meta-steps
use hard-output masking to preserve the straight-through path; ordinary steps
use gather/scatter. Router features are produced by the actual sparse
trajectory, not by a separate dense FFN planning pass. Functional AdamW,
hypergradients, budget rounding, padding exclusion, state detachment, and
masked/gathered forward equivalence were checked before training
(Appendix~\ref{app:validation}).

\subsection{A frozen low-max-abs allocation program}
\label{sec:program-method}
The second mechanism has no learned router. It computes
\begin{equation}
s_{t,\ell}=-\max_d |h_{t,\ell,d}|
\label{eq:lowmaxabs}
\end{equation}
from the current on-policy pre-FFN activation and executes the exact top-$B$
rows. Consequently, \emph{low}-activation tokens execute and high-activation
tokens are skipped. The score requires no labels, gradient estimation,
lookahead reward, or additional FFN pass.

We screened ten simple activation and position rules for 50 steps on bodies 4
and 8 at 37.5\% and 50\% activity. We advanced the strongest mean performer
among rules that beat random in every screening regime, froze
Eq.~\eqref{eq:lowmaxabs}, and then ran 200-step development and fresh-to-program
replication. Bodies 9 and 10 were not used for program selection, but had been
used in earlier unrelated experiments; they are not a globally untouched
benchmark.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Local signals fail for specific, limited reasons}
\label{sec:local-results}
At 11M, the entropy gate is nearly constant across difficulty bins, the
tag-initialized gate reverses its initial ordering, and the unsupervised head
is vacuous. Tag supervision produces a gate--difficulty correlation of 0.95,
yet paired gate-gradient-minus-shuffled accuracy differences are $-0.3$,
$-0.1$, and $-0.0$ percentage points. The online gate-gradient allocator also
loses to random windows (held-out loss 2.253 versus 1.274, one seed). These
outcomes reject the tested local procedures, but not other learning objectives.
The first-order allocator is a local surrogate, not an oracle for finite masks
or retraining. The relaxed-gate results are retained as diagnostics in
Appendix~\ref{app:legacy}, without an executable-savings interpretation.

\paragraph{Allocation controls.}
Shuffling the frozen scores preserves their distribution but degrades final
loss by 0.02530 on average, so token association matters. Strictly lagged
gradient rules and an Adam-preconditioned directional estimator also beat
random, but remain behind low-max-abs (Table~\ref{tab:ablations}). Thus the
frozen rule does not simply reproduce either tested form of immediate
first-order saliency. Its mechanism remains open: redundancy, update
suppression, curriculum effects, and sparse co-adaptation are candidates.

\subsection{The dense-training boundary remains}
\label{sec:practical}
A win over random at a fixed executed FFN budget is not a practical efficiency
result. The online router requires 20 higher-order updates, each evaluating
four reward batches. Its advantage disappears under dense evaluation, and it
did not beat dense-fewer-steps in a validated cost-matched comparison. The
frozen rule is much cheaper, but its cost-matched comparisons also did not beat
dense-fewer-steps.

We additionally tested a variant with dense FFN forward passes, partial FFN
backward computation, and an exponential moving average of weights. Under
corrected forward-and-backward accounting, development body 6 produced a
0.000366 loss win over dense, but the unchanged protocol lost by 0.003133 on
body 7 (Appendix~\ref{app:frontier}). Other development variants---dense
anchors, warmup or terminal dense corrections, gradient scaling, and output
calibration---did not establish a replicated win. Practical superiority over
dense-fewer-steps therefore remains undemonstrated.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\paragraph{What the evidence establishes.}
Useful token--layer FFN allocation is learnable in the tested mid-training
continuations. A router can acquire that allocation through realized-update
feedback, and a fixed activation statistic can also exploit it. The controlled
failures and positive results together identify a boundary between allocation
objectives, without establishing a universal law about adaptive computation.

\paragraph{What remains unresolved.}
First, the main positive results use 11M models on tagged arithmetic, 200-step
continuations from shared checkpoints, and one fixed held-out set. The 150M
evidence is only a 50-step, two-body, one-budget pilot with a new held-out set.
It passes the final sparse-loss comparison, but random has the larger
sparse-loss reduction during continuation. We have not shown a 200-step result
at 150M, from-initialization learning, natural-language transfer, or
independent held-out-set replication of the 200-step findings. Second, the
meta-router has only two confirmation seeds. Bodies 9 and 10, and the 150M
bodies, were not used to select the frozen rule but had appeared in earlier
unrelated experiments. Third, the frozen rule was selected on development
bodies and its mechanism is not yet understood. Fourth, current meta-learning
overhead is substantial, and no method has demonstrated a replicated
quality--total-cost advantage over dense training. Wall-clock microbenchmarks
do not repair that missing comparison.

\paragraph{Audit consequences.}
Our audit identified unsupported scale claims, an invalid MoD implementation,
affected saved-mask evaluations, and inconsistent cost accounting. We withdrew
the affected claims and comparisons, including the starved-150M causal result
and the 1.4B total-FLOP interpretation. Appendix~\ref{app:audit} details each
correction. The new claims rely on executable hard-budget experiments and
corrected accounting rather than those legacy comparisons.

each layer. It minimizes cross-entropy, a target-matching squared error, and a
mean-budget penalty. The body learning rate is $10^{-2}$ times the head rate,
and all forks share a Stage-1 checkpoint. Table~\ref{tab:relaxed} preserves
the central 11M comparison as a \emph{continuous relaxation}. The gate values
rank tokens, but they do not execute row skipping and cannot support an
executed-FLOP claim.

The gate-gradient target is
$-\sum_\ell \partial\mathcal L/\partial g_{t,\ell}$ evaluated at $g=1$.

only for the corresponding infinitesimal linearized problem. It is not an
upper bound on finite masks, nonlinear loss, another allocator, or retraining.

\subsection{Why dense inference was studied}
The earlier random FFN-dropout experiments asked whether intermittent FFN
updates could serve as a training-time regularizer while restoring the full
network for inference. That is a legitimate dense-deployment diagnostic, but
it is not an adaptive-inference method. Table~\ref{tab:nlquality} retains only
same-step held-out quality. It removes the previous total-FLOP-saving and
end-to-end-speedup interpretation. The separate gather benchmark measured a
kernel-level implementation, not the masked training driver used for these
runs.

\section{Protocol and Validation Details}
\label{app:validation}

\paragraph{Online router.}
The router applies LayerNorm and a linear map to each token state, concatenates
16-dimensional layer and position embeddings, and applies a two-layer MLP with
hidden width 128. Meta-updates occur at steps 1, 11, \ldots, 191. The body uses
AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019decoupled}; the router uses learning rate $10^{-3}$.
The development body was seed 4. Confirmation used bodies 8 and 9 with online
seeds 9208 and 9209 and otherwise unchanged settings. Reward batches are
subsequently body-trained, and this ordering was checked programmatically.

\paragraph{Frozen program.}
The development screen included RMS, mean-absolute, maximum-absolute, position,
and two-term outlier scores in both orientations. The selection rule advanced
the strongest mean step-50 improvement among programs that beat random in all
four screening regimes. Long development used bodies 4 and 8; replication used
bodies 9 and 10. Online seeds followed $9400+\text{body seed}$. Both used
37.5\% and 50\% exact per-layer activity. Shuffling permutes valid scores within
a sequence and layer while preserving the active count.

\paragraph{150M scale pilot.}
The frozen pilot used existing 4{,}000-step checkpoints for bodies 0 and 1,
tagged-v2 data, sequence length 512, and 50 continuation steps. Micro-batches
of 8 with four-step gradient accumulation preserved effective batch 32. The
body learning rate was $10^{-5}$, weight decay 0.1, and dropout 0.1. Online
seeds were 15000 and 15001. Evaluations at steps 0, 25, and 50 used 128
sequences per mode from newly generated held-out seed 20261115. Paired arms
had identical realized activity histories. The primary comparison was final
sparse loss on each body; no settings were changed afterward.

\paragraph{Validation checks.}
The functional optimizer was compared numerically with an ordinary AdamW update,
including optimizer moments. Hypergradients were compared with central finite
differences on a small soft-gate model. Straight-through masks were checked for
finite nonzero router gradients, exact rounded budgets, and zero padding
allocations. Hard masked and gathered forward evaluations were compared for
equivalence. Installed body and optimizer states were checked to be detached.
The fixed-program checks covered score shape, padding, exact budgets, and
selection semantics. Sparse-update primitives were separately compared with
autograd for outputs, input gradients, and parameter gradients.

\paragraph{Cost accounting.}
Allocation-learnability comparisons use equal updates and realized active-row
budgets. Practical comparisons additionally require attention per layer,
forward and backward work, routing, reward evaluations, meta-updates, and any
averaging or calibration. We found that PyTorch's MPS counter omitted standard
backward FLOPs in our audit. Corrected practical experiments therefore use
CPU-calibrated analytical counts rather than older MPS-only totals. The dense
batch-32 forward-and-backward reference is 580{,}235{,}821{,}056 FLOPs per step.
Feature scoring, selection, gather/scatter, and auxiliary operations are added
separately where applicable.

\section{Practical-Frontier Stress Test}
\label{app:frontier}

The closest development result did not come from sparse inference. It used a
dense FFN forward pass but only 87.5\% of token rows in FFN backward, with the
same low-max-abs score. This preserves dense activations and ordinary dense
inference while reducing parameter- and input-gradient matrix multiplications.
An exponential moving average of weights with decay 0.95 was evaluated for all
arms. Low-max-abs and random ran 105 and 106 steps respectively; dense ran 100.
All costs include dense forward computation and EMA work. For this variant,
the analytical step cost is
\begin{equation}
C=C_{\mathrm{dense}}-8N_{\mathrm{inactive}}d_{\mathrm{model}}d_{\mathrm{FFN}},
\end{equation}
where $N_{\mathrm{inactive}}$ counts inactive token--layer rows. CPU checks
confirmed that the formula matched full backward execution.
