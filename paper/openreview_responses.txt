===== GLOBAL REVISION COMMENT =====
BEGIN

Thank you for the detailed and specific criticisms. We have substantially revised both the claims and the experiments.

The revised paper no longer claims that useful token-level allocation is generally impossible to learn. It also no longer treats the first-order gate-gradient rule as an oracle or upper bound. We now test a router and model that adapt together during training. The router learns from the loss after the actual weight update caused by its token selections. We separately test a fixed rule based on token activations. Both improve over random masks that execute the same number of token rows in the tested 11M continuation setting.

We also corrected how computation is executed and counted. Fractional gates are now described only as mathematical relaxations: they still require the FFN computation. The new allocation experiments instead use binary on/off masks and compute only the selected token rows. We report evaluation with FFN skipping separately from evaluation of the full dense model. Practical comparisons count attention in every layer, forward and backward computation, and additional router work where applicable. We have withdrawn the previous 1.4B total-FLOP interpretation, the unsupported starved-150M causal claim, and the invalid MoD numerical comparison.

Finally, neither new allocation method has demonstrated a replicated win over training a dense model for fewer steps at matched total cost. The revised contribution is limited evidence about when useful token allocation can be learned, together with the original failure analysis and implementation corrections. It is not a claim of universal non-learnability or superior practical efficiency.

END


===== REVIEWER 32DR =====
BEGIN

Thank you for the direct questions. We acknowledge all three concerns: the new methods had not been tested at a second model size, the paper did not justify the practical value of a recipe that loses to dense-fewer-steps, and the broad headline about allocation failure conflicted with successful adaptive-computation methods. We have narrowed the claims, removed the affected comparisons, and added positive allocation experiments.

1. Is there a practical advantage over dense-fewer-steps?

The direct answer is no: we have not demonstrated a replicated setting in which our recipe is better than training a dense model for fewer steps at matched total cost.

The closest result came from a variant that kept the FFN forward pass dense but skipped some FFN backward computation. It also used an exponential moving average of the model weights. On model 6, which was used during development, this variant gave a 0.000366 loss advantage over dense-fewer-steps. When we applied the unchanged protocol to model 7, however, it lost by 0.003133. The average across the two models was +0.0014 loss relative to dense. We report this failed replication rather than calling the first result a practical win.

Random token-level FFN dropout is therefore retained as a diagnostic training intervention, not recommended as a generally superior recipe. The 1.35x result is now described only as a short kernel-level timing test, not as end-to-end acceleration of the original training driver. Wins over random or shuffled masks are used only as evidence that token selection matters. Dense-fewer-steps is the main practical baseline, and the abstract and discussion explicitly state that practical superiority remains undemonstrated.

We agree that a dominated dropout recipe alone would have weak practical motivation. The revised paper instead studies the boundary between three findings: simple local allocation rules fail; better-designed rules can make useful token selections; and neither has yet beaten dense-fewer-steps in a replicated total-cost comparison.

2. How does this relate to MoD?

We agree that MoD's published success contradicts a universal claim that token-adaptive computation cannot work. It does not contradict the narrower observation that the particular signals and training rules we tested failed. Our original headline improperly combined those two statements.

We also found an implementation error affecting the intended causal computation in our MoD baseline. We have therefore withdrawn its numerical comparison and do not use it to argue either for or against the published method. The revision discusses MoD, Mixture-of-Recursions, and related recurrent models as positive evidence for adaptive computation.

More importantly, we tested whether better designs could succeed in our own setting. One router learns from the loss after the actual AdamW update caused by its token mask. It improves final loss, measured with the same FFN skipping used during training, over random by -0.0366 and -0.0636 on two confirmation models. It also beats shuffled-score controls throughout training.

A second method is a fixed rule that executes tokens with the smallest maximum absolute activations entering each FFN. After the rule was selected, we left it unchanged and compared it with random masks executing the same number of token rows. It won all eight comparisons across four models and two activity budgets. Its mean loss improvement was -0.0319 +/- 0.0062, and its accuracy improvement was +3.37 +/- 0.65 percentage points.

These results change our conclusion from "allocation is not learnable" to "the original local procedures fail, but useful allocation can be learned under these specified alternatives." They still do not establish an advantage over dense-fewer-steps.

3. What experimental support and scale evidence are now available?

The router experiment includes two confirmation models that were not used to develop that router. The fixed-rule experiment includes four models at two activity budgets, including models that were not used to select the rule. We separately report evaluation with FFN skipping and evaluation of the full dense model, including the unfavorable dense-evaluation results.

These are new 11M continuation experiments. We do not describe the 1.4B three-thousand-step fine-tune as pretraining or claim that it validates the new methods.

For the original starved-150M arm, our audit found that the experiment driver ignored the --size argument. The claimed replication was therefore unsupported. We have withdrawn it rather than arguing about whether its gaps would remain after adding noise estimates. None of the revised positive claims relies on that experiment.

Evidence at a second model size remains a separate requirement. Any 150M continuation follow-up will be reported with its exact models, controls, continuation length, and held-out data. It will not be presented as a repair of the invalid original causal arm.

We would value your view on which issue is most important for the remaining discussion: the lack of second-scale evidence for these new methods, or the lack of a replicated win over dense-fewer-steps. We recognize that these are distinct limitations and do not want to conflate them.

Thank you for quoting the exact passages and for separating the writing, computation, and deployment concerns. We acknowledge each of them. The two quoted contribution paragraphs have been removed. The dense-inference tables no longer claim inference efficiency. The invalid numerical MoD comparison has been withdrawn. The recurrent adaptive-computation papers you cited are now discussed. The universal non-learnability wording has also been removed. Below we focus on the three issues that required substantial changes: the writing, the meaning of the gate and its FLOPs, and the missing test of better allocation designs.

1. Writing and organization

We agree that the original contribution statement was too compressed to be useful, even to a careful reader familiar with the area. We have rewritten the title, abstract, introduction, contributions, methods, results, and limitations rather than only editing the two quoted bullets.

The revised paper is organized around three separate questions. First, do the tested local signals produce useful token allocation? Second, can a different learning objective or a fixed rule make better token selections under a hard execution budget? Third, does any benefit survive a total-cost comparison with dense-fewer-steps?

This replaces the previous mixture of workshop history, accounting terminology, and scale claims in the contribution paragraphs. The method section now explains soft-gate diagnostics, binary execution masks, and the shared training protocol before presenting results. The revised title is "When Is Token-Level Compute Allocation Learnable? Realized Updates, Frozen Rules, and the Dense-Training Boundary."

2. Eq. (1), continuous gates, and actual FLOPs

Your criticism is correct when Eq. (1) is interpreted as actual computation. For x3 = LN(x2 + g * FFN(x2)), with g in [0,1], any nonzero fractional g still requires the FFN forward and backward computation. Multiplying the output by 0.5 does not reduce the matrix-multiplication cost by 50%. The original billing described a mathematical relaxation, not executed savings. We now say this explicitly. The soft-gate results are retained only as diagnostics of gate ordering and target content.

The new allocation experiments work differently. In each layer, a score selects a fixed number of token rows, B. A binary on/off mask then selects exactly those rows. The implementation gathers the selected rows, runs the FFN only on them, and places their outputs back into the full sequence. Other rows receive no FFN contribution. Padding tokens are excluded, and attention remains dense.

Thus 50% activity means that half of the valid token-layer FFN rows execute. It does not mean that total training FLOPs fall by 50%. We make no executable-savings claim from a fractional gate value. The fixed-rule training and ordinary router-training steps use this actual row-skipping path. The periodic router-learning updates are described and counted separately.

We agree that the original Eq. (1) and billing sentence could not support a realized-savings claim. The revised execution claims instead depend on binary masks and actual row skipping.

3. The original conclusion was too broad; we tested better designs directly

We agree that the original results might be specific to the architecture and learning design. A gate gradient with the model held fixed estimates only the effect of an extremely small gate change. It does not predict the effect of turning an FFN off, changing the mask by a finite amount, or continuing to train the model and allocator together. We removed the oracle and upper-bound language and no longer infer general non-learnability from that procedure.

More importantly, we ran two positive tests of the alternative you suggested.

The first test trains a router and model together for 200 continuation steps. Every ten steps, we measure the model weight update caused by the router's mask and evaluate the updated model on the next four ordinary training batches. The router learns by differentiating through that weight update. In other words, it is trained on the observed consequence of its token selections rather than on a gate-gradient target.

Here, "sparse loss" means loss measured with the same 50% FFN execution used during training. On model 8, final sparse losses are 1.2600 for the learned router, 1.2967 for random masks, and 1.2982 for shuffled scores. The learned-router-minus-random difference is -0.0366. On model 9, the corresponding losses are 1.2343, 1.2979, and 1.3000, giving a difference of -0.0636. These confirmation models were not used to develop the router.

Averaged across the two models, the learned-router-minus-random difference is -0.0501 +/- 0.0135 standard error. The learned-router-minus-shuffled difference is -0.0519 +/- 0.0138 standard error. The learned router is ahead of both controls at steps 50, 100, 150, and 200 in both models. This directly shows that a different training design can learn useful token allocation where the original local-gradient rule failed.

The second test uses a fixed rule rather than a learned router. For each token, the rule measures the largest absolute activation value entering the FFN. Tokens with the smallest such values execute. After development selection, we left this rule unchanged. It beats random masks executing the same number of rows in all eight model-budget combinations and at all 32 evaluations across four models and two activity budgets.

Averaging first within each model, the fixed rule improves final sparse loss by -0.0319 +/- 0.0062 and answer accuracy by +3.37 +/- 0.65 percentage points. Shuffling the scores among tokens, while keeping the same number of rows active, worsens loss by 0.02530 on average. This shows that the relationship between the scores and particular tokens matters.

These results do not establish that every adaptive-computation design works, and they do not show practical superiority over dense-fewer-steps. They do show that the original design-dependent failures should not have been generalized into a non-learnability claim.

4. Dense inference and related work

The original random FFN-dropout experiments asked whether intermittently skipping FFN updates during training could act as a regularizer while restoring the full network at inference. That is a limited training-time question, not an adaptive-inference method. We now label those tables as same-step quality comparisons, retain the unfavorable dense-fewer result, and make no inference-savings claim from them.

For the new experiments, evaluation with FFN skipping and evaluation of the full dense model are reported separately. The learned router's improvement with FFN skipping does not translate into a better dense model: dense evaluation is worse than random by 0.0499 loss on average.

Our numerical MoD baseline was withdrawn after we found the implementation's causality error. We do not use that invalid comparison to argue against the published MoD result. The revision discusses MoD and Mixture-of-Recursions as positive evidence that adaptive computation can work under appropriate designs. It also discusses Moosa et al.: their finding that an interpretable allocation pattern need not improve algorithmic generalization matches our distinction between producing an allocation pattern and obtaining a useful model outcome.

We would appreciate continuing the discussion on two specific points: does the binary-mask and row-skipping description resolve the FLOP concern, and does the experiment through actual model updates address your hypothesis that a better learning design can make allocation useful? If the revised presentation remains unclear, a pointer to the first ambiguous passage would be especially useful.

===== REVIEWER rfcv =====
BEGIN

Thank you for identifying the exact gaps between the original experiments and its claims. We acknowledge all three questions directly. There is no formal nonlinear bound from the first-order procedure. The old starved-150M claim was unsupported and has been withdrawn. The original efficiency comparisons did not consistently combine total cost, actual execution, and training behavior. The largest changes are a new experiment in which the model and router adapt together, a positive result from a fixed allocation rule, and a strict separation of learnability from practical efficiency.

1. What the first-order result does and does not establish

There is no formal argument that sorting gate gradients bounds nonlinear allocation or learning performance. The target -sum_l dL/dg_{t,l}, evaluated at g=1, measures how sensitive the loss is to a very small gate change with the model held fixed. Sorting this quantity is optimal only for that small-change linear approximation. It does not bound the loss after turning FFNs on or off, choosing a different allocation rule, training the model and router together, or retraining. We removed "oracle" and "upper bound" for this procedure and narrowed the conclusion to the tested approximation and update rule.

We agree that adding seeds to the same 100-step allocator would only measure its failure more precisely. It would not answer your main objection. We therefore changed both the allocation method and the replication structure.

2. Training the router on the actual consequence of its mask

The new router and model train together for 200 updates from a shared mid-training checkpoint. All allocation arms receive the same pregenerated training batches and execute the same number of FFN token rows. The router reads the current activations immediately before each FFN and selects exactly 50% of valid token rows.

Every ten steps, we compute the model weight update caused by that mask and evaluate the updated model on the next four ordinary training batches. The router learns by differentiating through this weight update and reducing the observed post-update loss. Those four batches are subsequently used for ordinary model training, so the method does not introduce extra training examples. Their additional evaluations and the backward computation needed to train the router through the weight update are counted as additional computation. The updated model weights and optimizer state become the actual next training state.

This addresses three specific gaps in the original experiment: the model now adapts together with the router; masks are refreshed during training rather than every 100 steps; and the learning signal comes from the actual finite weight update rather than a small gate change with the model held fixed.

Here, "sparse loss" means evaluation with the same 50% FFN execution used during training. On model 8, final sparse losses are 1.2600 for the learned router, 1.2967 for random masks, and 1.2982 for shuffled scores. Learned-router-minus-random is -0.0366, and learned-router-minus-shuffled is -0.0382. On model 9, the losses are 1.2343, 1.2979, and 1.3000, giving differences of -0.0636 and -0.0657. These confirmation models were not used to develop the router.

Across the two models, the mean learned-router-minus-random difference is -0.0501 +/- 0.0135 standard error. The mean learned-router-minus-shuffled difference is -0.0519 +/- 0.0138 standard error. The learned router is ahead of both controls at steps 50, 100, 150, and 200 in each model. The result is therefore not a single favorable final evaluation. We interpret it as a limited positive counterexample to the original broad non-learnability interpretation, not as evidence that all allocation methods work.

3. Additional evidence from a cheap fixed rule

We also tested whether a simple rule based on current token activations already contains useful allocation structure. For each token, the selected rule takes the largest absolute activation value entering the FFN. Tokens with the smallest such values execute, up to an exact per-layer budget. The rule needs no learned router, future reward evaluation, gradient estimate, or additional dense FFN pass.

The rule was selected on development models and then left unchanged. Across four models and activity budgets of 37.5% and 50%, it wins all eight final comparisons against random masks executing the same number of rows. It also wins all 32 evaluations at steps 50, 100, 150, and 200. Averaging first within each model, the final loss difference is -0.0319 +/- 0.0062 and the answer-accuracy difference is +3.37 +/- 0.65 percentage points. Models 9 and 10 were not used to select the rule, although they had appeared in earlier unrelated experiments.

The shuffled control keeps the same scores and active-row count within each layer but assigns those scores to different tokens. It is worse than the fixed rule by 0.02530 loss on average. Rules based on previous-step gradients or on Adam-adjusted gradient direction also outperform random, but remain behind the fixed activation rule in all four development settings. These controls show that assigning computation to particular tokens matters, rather than merely executing a fixed fraction of them.

4. Separating token-selection quality from dense-model quality and total cost

We now separate three claims.

First, token selection under a fixed execution budget. The comparisons above use equal training updates and equal numbers of executed FFN rows. They test whether the choice of tokens matters; they are not total-cost victories.

Second, quality of the full dense model. The learned router's benefit is specific to evaluation with FFN skipping. When the resulting models are evaluated densely, the learned-router model is worse than random by 0.0499 loss on average. Even its sparse loss remains 0.0240 above a model trained densely for the same number of updates. We therefore do not claim that the router learns a better dense model.

Third, practical total cost. The original 1.4B counter counted attention once rather than once per layer, so we withdrew that total-FLOP interpretation. We also found that PyTorch's counter on Apple MPS omitted ordinary backward-pass work. Old MPS-only totals are therefore not used for practical claims. Revised practical comparisons use operation counts checked against CPU execution, including forward and backward computation in every layer and additional work such as routing, reward evaluations, router updates, or weight averaging where applicable.

5. Row-skipping validation and the throughput benchmark

We agree that the original text connected two paths that should have been distinguished: the original training driver, which multiplied outputs by masks, and a separate timing benchmark, which actually skipped rows.

The revised validation checks that binary masking and row skipping produce equivalent forward outputs, that the selected row counts are exact and exclude padding, and that the differentiable weight-update implementation matches ordinary AdamW including optimizer moments. We also checked gradients through the router-learning update against finite differences, and checked the outputs, input gradients, and parameter gradients of the sparse-update operations against autograd.

The fixed-rule training and ordinary router-training steps use actual row skipping. The periodic router-learning step uses a differentiable approximation around the binary mask; it is distinguished from ordinary execution and its additional work is counted.

These checks establish numerical properties of the operations used in the new experiments. We do not claim that they establish end-to-end equivalence of the original masked training driver and the separate timing benchmark. The 1.35x figure is now described only as a short kernel-level benchmark, not as end-to-end training acceleration.

6. Dense-fewer-steps and scale remain explicit limitations

We have not established a replicated setting in which any proposed recipe outperforms shorter dense training at matched actual cost. Under the corrected accounting, a variant with dense forward computation and partial backward computation gave a 0.000366 loss advantage on one development model. The unchanged protocol lost by 0.003133 on the second model. The two-model mean difference is +0.0014 loss relative to dense. We report both outcomes and make no practical-superiority claim.

For scale, the original starved-150M causal claim was withdrawn because its experiment driver ignored --size. None of the new positive claims depends on it. The new multi-model results establish replication within the tested 11M continuation setting, not second-scale generality. We will report any 150M continuation result separately with its exact protocol rather than treating it as validation of the invalid original arm.

We hope this separates the two claims that were previously conflated. Does the new evidence from actual mask-dependent weight updates address your concern about learnability, independently of practical efficiency? If a soundness concern remains, would it be more useful to discuss the difference between sparse and dense evaluation, or the total-cost comparison, first?
